Chaos

A dynamic system is an ordinary differential equation with several spatial dimensions. These examples are all first order equations in two spatial dimensions, which means the position of a point completely determines its velocity.

x′(t)=fx(x(t),y(t),t)y′(t)=fy(x(t),y(t),t) \begin{aligned} x'(t) &= f_x(x(t), y(t), t) \\ y'(t) &= f_y(x(t), y(t), t) \end{aligned}

You can think of the field being a flow of water. We are going to drop some motes of dust into the water and watch where they flow. This is not a great analogy because water is not very compressible, and these flows are.

The first few examples are quite simple. The last one shows some interesting behavior. Scroll to the bottom for some recommended reading.

Isoclines

Turn on the “Iso” checkbox to display the isoclines (also called nullclines). These are the curves where one component of the velocity is zero:

  • On the xx-isocline (x′=0x' = 0), horizontal motion stops and the flow is purely vertical. Crossing this curve is where the flow changes between moving left and moving right.

  • On the yy-isocline (y′=0y' = 0), vertical motion stops and the flow is purely horizontal. Crossing this curve is where the flow changes between moving up and moving down.

Isoclines are useful because they divide the plane into regions where the flow moves in a consistent direction (up-left, up-right, down-left, or down-right). Wherever an xx-isocline and a yy-isocline cross, both x′=0x' = 0 and y′=0y' = 0, marking a stationary point (equilibrium) where a mote stays still.
